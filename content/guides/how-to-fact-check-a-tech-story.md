Tech news moves fast, and a lot of what goes viral is half true, out of date or made up. You don't need to be a journalist to check it. Here's a five-step, five-minute routine. It's a simplified version of the rules [DarkWire runs on every story](/about).

## Minute 1: Find the actual claim

Strip away the hype and write the claim as one plain sentence:

- Viral post: *"This new AI just made every developer obsolete 😳"*
- Actual claim: *"A company released a new AI coding tool."*

Half the time, this step alone deflates the story. Watch for words like **"basically," "just," "everyone,"** and **"never"**. They usually mean the claim got stretched.

## Minute 2: Go to the source

Search the claim with the key names (the company, the product, the person) and look for:

1. **The primary source:** the company's own blog or press release, the research paper, the court filing, the government announcement.
2. **Established newsrooms:** outlets with named reporters and a corrections policy.

If the only places saying it are anonymous accounts, screenshots and aggregator pages, it's **unconfirmed**, no matter how many times you've seen it.

## Minute 3: Check the date and the numbers

- **Is it new?** Old stories get recycled as "breaking" all the time. Check the publish date on the original.
- **Are the numbers right?** Viral posts round up, mix up millions and billions, or confuse revenue with profit. Find the figure in the original source and compare.
- **Is it a forecast or a fact?** "Could reach $1 trillion" is a prediction, not a result.

## Minute 4: Read the qualifiers

Good reporting tells you how sure it is. Learn to read the signals:

| Phrase | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| "announced" / "confirmed" | The company or official said it on the record |
| "reportedly" / "according to sources" | The outlet heard it but hasn't independently confirmed it |
| "alleges" / "claims" | One side's accusation, not a proven fact |
| "is said to be" / "rumored" | Weakest: treat as speculation |

For anything involving **drama, lawsuits or politics**, look for **two independent outlets** or an official statement before you treat it as solid.

## Minute 5: Check for the screenshot trap

Screenshots of posts, headlines and chats are trivially easy to fake or crop out of context. Before you trust one:

- Find the **original post or article** it claims to show.
- Check the account is the **real one**, not a look-alike handle.
- If it's an image or video, run a quick **reverse image search** (see our guide on [spotting AI-generated images](/guides/how-to-spot-ai-generated-images)).

## When you can't confirm it

That's an answer too. "Unconfirmed" is a perfectly good reason not to share something. If it's real, it'll be confirmed soon, and you'll have lost nothing by waiting.

## The bottom line

Claim, source, date and numbers, qualifiers, screenshots. Five steps, five minutes. Do it a few times and it becomes automatic, and you'll be the person in the group chat who's always right.
